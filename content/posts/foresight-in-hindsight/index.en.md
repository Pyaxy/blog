I've been learning Japanese on Duolingo lately, and it's going pretty well. Apart from the time and effort it takes to get used to and memorize the fifty-sound kana chart, it's been fairly easy to get started. But why was learning English so painful for me in primary school?

When I first started English in third grade, I scored just 46. I still remember that score. I don't know whether it was an issue with the English teacher or whether things just hadn't clicked for me yet. I kept muddling my way through it, and I tried tutoring classes along the way, but my grades were only average. At least in my own mind back then, my English was below average.

In my second year of high school, something suddenly seemed to click. I figured out how to learn English and gained some confidence in it. I did pretty well in English on the gaokao, and later passed CET-4 and CET-6 without much trouble too.

But here's the strange part. I feel like I studied English for over a decade just to reach a point where I can just about understand 80% of a technical document. Before I started Japanese, I once thought it might take me another decade or more to get Japanese to the level my English is at now. Yet getting started with Japanese has actually been quick. Why? Is it because Japanese already has some similarities with Chinese, or because I have experience learning a language now?

---

### I think the latter is the main reason

The similarities between Chinese and Japanese certainly make for a comfortable start. Especially since Japanese looks like Chinese and sounds like English, it really does help you pick up some basic words quickly.

When I was little and learning English, teachers would explain things like `subject–verb–object` and the `third-person singular` and have us memorize them. But to me, this was no different from memorizing classical poems. The teacher's assignment was to remember these things, so I remembered them. What did they actually mean? No idea. But memorized = assignment completed.

After all these years of figuring things out, hitting walls, and learning, I've gradually gotten a handle on something I'll tentatively call `meta-learning`, or `meta`. The word is abstract, but abstraction is exactly what it does. You can abstract existing, general-purpose `pattern`s up one level, then keep abstracting the resulting level. I'll tentatively call this phenomenon `meta-recursion`. This rule shows up quite often in everyday life.

The pattern I've abstracted from language learning is to organize a language into something like a knowledge graph. Each node is an individual word, phrase, or grammar point, but they connect to one another and together form a graph.

This actually helped me see the limitations of how I used to learn English. Back then, I thought of English as isolated pieces. A word was a word, a sentence was a sentence, and a phrase was a phrase. The only connection between one word and another was that this one appeared on page one and that one appeared on page two. Beyond that, they had nothing to do with each other. Still, memorizing words = memorizing textbook passages.

**But I don't deny just how much memorizing vocabulary helps with learning English.**

---

### Memorizing vocabulary can greatly improve your ability to build a knowledge graph

It can help you build a knowledge graph very quickly, but in the early stages, you see almost no return at all. Only when the amount of vocabulary you've accumulated passes a certain point do the returns start showing up rapidly.

And the period when my vocabulary grew rapidly was precisely my second year of high school. Even then, it took a teacher at a tutoring school to drill it into me.

Her teaching method was unusual, but it took a lot of effort. Every year, she would work through all the past gaokao papers. Once she'd finished, she'd write down whatever she thought would help with that year's exam. Phrases, good sentences, words, words that rarely came up on the gaokao but were actually used a lot in everyday life—she recorded all of them and added them to the knowledge she'd already compiled.

Most importantly, she would build an `obsidian`-style knowledge graph in her head. Working through the past papers every year helped with this too: a paper from ten years ago would have been worked through ten times over those ten years, so she knew every point inside out. This was also where she was better than other teachers. An ordinary teacher would see a word in class and think of the exam points associated with that word.

In her classes, as soon as she started with a word, she would instantly activate the subgraph around it. From that word, she'd go through words with the same prefix or suffix, synonyms, antonyms, words with similar spellings, and then all the different forms of those words, noun forms, verb forms, and so on. Then those words would form phrases and sentences, followed by how they were used in everyday English. She'd fill a whole blackboard. I feel like if you gave her unlimited time, she could write out everything there was to know about gaokao English.

Her only homework was this: take notes and copy everything down during class, memorize it afterward, and get checked the next class. And she was strict. If you hadn't memorized it, she'd scold you. I was pretty scared of her back then and didn't want to get scolded, so I memorized the notes after every class. I was also the kind of person who, when a daytime class got boring, would take out the notebook and look through it. I wasn't even trying to memorize it all. The focus was on maintaining the lines between the points she'd taught: why mentioning this word could bring that word to mind. Even that was just so I could memorize things faster and avoid getting scolded.

And back then, my knowledge graph was incomplete. Every new point I memorized meant creating a new node. Node reuse, or cache hits, was rare, which made it painful. But the more I memorized, the more I realized how efficient this was. It also solved the problem of my previous English knowledge being atomized. Along the way, I memorized the vocabulary on the gaokao syllabus too. Of course, filling in the gaps also meant adding words beyond the syllabus. This was what got me straight through CET-4 and CET-6, along with everyday English phrases. Otherwise, all the English I'd learned would have been written English, and I wouldn't be able to talk to people in conversational English.

The hard part of this method is getting started. When the graph is incomplete and the nodes are isolated, it's hard to feel how the graph helps with English. Of course there's no motivation to do something that gives you no feedback. This teacher happened to bridge that difficult early accumulation stage, helping you build up quantitative change until it became qualitative change. I was lucky to meet a teacher like that.

Once that qualitative change happened, the internal loop formed naturally. I would actively organize knowledge into the graph myself. I no longer needed to memorize only the words and points she gave me. I felt that any useful piece of knowledge could go into my own graph. And I no longer needed to make a deliberate effort to memorize vocabulary. That task had turned into `create a node -> add it to the graph -> maintain the index regularly`.

So overall, the whole difficulty I had with English came down to this: the initial learning method showed no return in the short term, which made it hard to stick with. Even when I first started memorizing her notes, I scoffed at it. I just thought it was no different from the vocabulary and textbook passages I'd been memorizing since I was little. Especially before someone has built an index for this graph, they'll only see isolated nodes, exactly like what their schoolteachers usually tell them to memorize. Naturally, they won't think much of it.

### Before they “start working,” some learning methods look almost exactly like ineffective ones

When I first started learning this graph method, what I saw was still: taking notes, memorizing words, memorizing phrases.

On the surface, it was no different from what we did at school. It was even more tiring. I'd think, “Isn't this still just memorizing vocabulary? It's not like I haven't done that before.”

The real difference only becomes visible once the knowledge network reaches a certain size. Before, memorizing 100 things meant 100 independent nodes that all needed maintaining. Later, when I learned the 101st thing, I might only need to find a place for it. I wouldn't even need to make a special effort to maintain it, because it would automatically connect to dozens of earlier nodes.

In other words, the return is delayed, while the cost is immediate. That's a bit at odds with human nature. People are always drawn to things that give feedback in real time. Short videos, games—complete an action and the feedback comes instantly. Even with a certain kind of fake effort, mechanically carrying out some actions and taking that to mean those actions are working, that feeling is itself a kind of immediate feedback. After all, action completed = I worked hard = I feel satisfied.

People often judge something new by its “current use,” but the real value of many things comes from their future uses.

I'll tentatively say this method has `foresight in hindsight`.

---

### Learning English itself has this quality too.

It was only after I learned English that I realized how much it could help someone understand the world. But maybe only people who have also learned English can relate to that realization.

Suppose I tell someone who's never learned English or tasted the benefits of the knowledge and information it gives you: “Learning English helps a lot. You can understand English documentation, papers, forums, Reddit, YouTube, and overseas news directly. It feels like the world has suddenly gotten a whole lot bigger.” They might just think, “Learning English is important. It'll be useful later.” Once again, it's a simple, repeated, Chinese-parent-style exhortation to study. Chinese students have heard those eight Chinese characters so often from kindergarten through high school that their ears have practically grown calluses.

It's not that they can't understand the sentence. They just don't have the experience to verify it. To their ears, it always sounds like a slogan. They've heard plenty of slogans; one more makes no difference. Only when they first run into a problem they can't solve on the Chinese internet, head into the English-speaking world, and do a quick search—only to find that a problem no one in China seems to have encountered has been encountered by so many people overseas, with a standard solution already available—can that sentence turn from lofty advice into: “Wait, that was actually true?”

The former is “I know English is useful.” The latter is “I can no longer imagine what it was like not to know English.”

---

### I guess this is one limitation of the Chinese way of urging people to study

The Chinese way of urging people to study isn't necessarily useless, but its limitation is that it keeps passing on conclusions:

- Learning English is useful.
- Reading more is useful.
- Learning math properly is useful.

But no one ever tells you what exactly it's useful for. Even if they do, you might not be able to put it to use yet. What's missing is still the actual experience behind that sentence.

As a student, you can't see `memorize vocabulary -> accumulate enough to build a graph -> learning new words gets easier -> reading becomes more accessible -> receive information firsthand -> gain more channels of information -> the world gets bigger`. All you can see is that tonight, you have all these words to memorize again.

---

### There are two kinds of knowledge

One can be told to you. The other can only be yours once you've experienced it.

In a world where life is filled with motivational platitudes, people are already tired of trying to verify whether any of it is true. They have to bear that uncertainty and pay a cost in exchange for a return that might not even exist. That teacher of mine bore this uncertainty for me. Before I'd verified whether any of this was useful, she went straight past the persuasion stage and made it `don't want to get scolded -> memorize vocabulary`. Once I'd checked it for myself and resolved the uncertainty, it became `there's something to this -> memorize vocabulary`.

But not every field has a teacher like that. A lot of valuable knowledge, once written down in a sentence, looks exactly like cheap motivational platitudes, disciplinary rhetoric from authority figures, or marketing spiel.

- Persistence brings victory.
- Suffering is a blessing.
- Education changes your destiny.

These sayings are true in some situations and are just fooling people in others. You simply can't tell from the sentence alone. Language can preserve a conclusion, but it can't preserve the experience that led to it. Like “only understanding what that saying means after a breakup,” “only understanding certain things your parents did after living independently,” or “only realizing why engineering standards are so fussy after working on a large project.”

### What you learn from paper is shallow; to truly understand it, you must put it into practice

I'm gradually coming to understand what this line means too. And the most interesting thing is that the line itself is on paper. There's a sense of recursion to it, and a sense of self-reference too.

> You have to experience it yourself to truly understand that “some things can only be understood through personal experience.”

Since practice is so important, a new question comes up.

### When a truth you haven't yet verified for yourself looks no different from motivational platitudes, disciplinary rhetoric, or fallacies, what should you do?

I haven't worked out an answer to this yet. I have a few ideas, but not many. For now, I suppose that if following a saying can help you gain more knowledge and develop more fully as a person, you can give it a try.

### An aside

This post is meant to break the “spell” of the foresight in hindsight that some practices have: to help people understand that not seeing a return yet doesn't necessarily mean the method is wrong. But the post itself is part of the “spell.” It, too, has recursion and self-reference.

I suppose it's a long version of “What you learn from paper is shallow; to truly understand it, you must put it into practice.” But it is, in the end, still on paper. People who understand my post naturally have relevant practical experience to support and verify it. People who don't might feel it's just another rehashed piece of motivational fluff. Basically, if you know, you know.

For those who don't, I hope this can at least leave a marker. I hope that a few years from now, when you've experienced something similar yourself, you'll think of this post.
